# Annotations and callouts

## The one-sentence version

An annotation is text or a mark on the chart itself that says what the reader is looking at, and a chart with the right annotation does not need a paragraph underneath it.

## What it is

Anything added on top of the plotted data to guide interpretation:

- **A finding label** — a short sentence placed near the relevant point or bar. "Launch, Mar 12" with an arrow to the spike.
- **Reference lines and bands** — a horizontal line at the target, the prior-year level, or the break-even point; a shaded band for a recession, a promo period, an outage.
- **Direct series labels** — the series name at the end of its line, replacing the legend.
- **Value labels** — the number on or next to the mark, when exact values matter.
- **The title** — the loudest annotation, and it should state the takeaway, not the topic.

## Why it exists

A chart shows a pattern; it does not explain why the pattern is there or what to do about it. The reader supplies that from memory, or from the surrounding text, or gets it wrong. An annotation puts the interpretation in the same visual field as the evidence, so the reader sees the spike and the reason for the spike at the same time, and the chart becomes self-contained. Self-contained charts survive being forwarded, screenshotted, and pasted into other decks without their context.

## How it works

**The title carries the message.** "Revenue by month" is a topic. "Revenue recovered to pre-outage levels within three weeks" is a finding. Write the title as the sentence you want repeated in the meeting. If the chart has a clear single takeaway, the title is where it goes.

**Label the thing, not everything.** One or two annotations per chart. Point at the spike, the crossover, the outlier, the moment something changed. Annotating every point is clutter and defeats the purpose (nothing stands out if everything is labelled).

**Reference lines give the data meaning.** A revenue line means little without a comparison. Add a faint horizontal line at last year's average, or the target, or zero, with a small label. Now "up" and "down" have a baseline. A shaded band behind the plot for a known event (a price change, a holiday, a system migration) lets the reader attribute the wiggle without you writing "note: prices changed in April" below.

**Direct labels beat legends.** A legend makes the reader look away from the data, match a color, look back, and remember. A label at the end of each line, in the line's color, removes all of that. Use a legend only when direct labels would overlap badly.

**Annotation style:**

- Text small and in a muted color, so it supports the data without competing with it. The exception is the one callout that is the point, which can be a touch heavier.
- Connect a label to its target with a thin line or a small arrow if the label cannot sit right next to it. Keep the connector short and unobtrusive.
- Position labels in whitespace, not on top of the data. Nudge them so they do not collide with gridlines, other labels, or the plotted marks.
- Never let an annotation obscure a data point it is describing.

## When you use it

On every chart with an audience. The workflow: make the chart, decide the one thing the reader should take away, write that as the title, then add one or two annotations that point at the evidence for it. Two minutes, and the chart stops needing a caption.

## A worked example

A line of weekly active users for a year. Unannotated, the reader sees a bumpy upward line and thinks "growing, I guess".

Annotated:

- Title: "Weekly actives up 40% after the onboarding redesign, holding the gain."
- A shaded vertical band over the two weeks of the redesign rollout, labelled "Onboarding v2".
- A short label at the point where the line steps up: "+40% over 4 weeks".
- A faint horizontal reference line at the pre-redesign average, labelled "pre-v2 baseline".
- The line itself is one color; no legend needed for a single series.

Now the reader sees the growth, sees when it started, sees how big it was against the old baseline, and sees the cause, all without reading anything below the chart.

> **Try This**
> Take a chart from a case analysis. Rewrite the title as the finding. Add exactly one callout pointing at the evidence, and one reference line giving the values a baseline. Remove the legend by labelling the series directly. Check the chart makes its point with the caption covered up.
