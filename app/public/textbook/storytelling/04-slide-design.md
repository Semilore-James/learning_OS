# Slide design for data

## The one-sentence version

A data slide has one chart, a title that states the takeaway, and almost nothing else, and the most common mistake is treating the slide as a container to fill rather than a single point to make.

## What it is

The layout of one slide in a data presentation:

- **The title** — a full sentence stating what this slide shows you should conclude. Not "Revenue by region". "West region drove 80% of the shortfall."
- **One chart** — the evidence for the title. Designed to the Visualization book's standards (sorted, one accent, honest axis, direct labels).
- **Optional: one annotation or a one-line "so what"** if the chart needs a pointer.
- **A source line**, small, at the bottom.
- Nothing else. No logo taking up a quarter of the space, no decorative sidebar, no three bullet points repeating the chart in words.

## Why it exists

The default slide, from every template, is a title placeholder and a big bulleted content box. Analysts fill the box with bullets and then squeeze a chart in next to them, and the result is a slide with two competing things to read and no clear point. A data slide should be scannable in three seconds: read the title, glance at the chart, got it.

## How it works

**The title carries the message.** Whoever reads only the titles (which is most people, later, from the emailed PDF) should get the whole argument. So each title is the takeaway sentence, written as a claim. This is the highest-leverage change you can make to a deck: rewrite every title from topic to takeaway.

**One chart per slide.** If you have two charts that belong together, either combine them into one (a small-multiple, a dual panel sharing an axis) or split into two slides. Two full charts on one slide means the audience does not know which to look at, and you have not decided what the slide is about.

**Kill the chartjunk.** On the chart: no 3D, no gradient fills, no drop shadows, no unnecessary gridlines, no legend if you can label directly, no decimal places the audience does not need. On the slide: no clip art, no full-bleed background image behind text, no animated transitions between bullet points.

**Size the chart to fill the slide.** A small chart floating in white space with bullets beside it is weak. The chart is the content; give it the room. Text supports it, not the other way around.

**Consistency across the deck:**

- Same fonts, same color meanings, same chart types for the same metrics on every slide.
- A template: title top, chart filling the body, source bottom-left, page number bottom-right. Every slide the same skeleton.
- If "West" is the accent color on slide 3, it is the accent color on slide 7.

**Text on a data slide:**

- The title (a sentence).
- Optionally one line of "so what" if the implication is not obvious from the chart.
- Annotations on the chart itself for specific points.
- That is the budget. If you are writing a paragraph, it belongs in the notes or a written document, not on the slide.

**Build for the medium.** Live presentation: minimal text, you narrate. Emailed document ("slideument"): the slide must stand alone, so a bit more text is acceptable, but resist turning it into a page of prose. If it genuinely needs to be a document, write a document.

## When you use it

When you turn the narrative arc (previous chapter) into actual slides. Each arc bullet is one slide: a takeaway title, one chart proving it, minimal support. Do a pass at the end where you read only the titles and check the story holds.

## A worked example

**Before:** a slide titled "Regional Performance Q3". Left half: five bullet points ("West was down", "East grew 6%", "margin pressure continued", ...). Right half: a small unsorted bar chart of revenue by region, rainbow colored, with a legend. The company logo occupies the top-left corner.

**After:** title "West's decline accounts for the entire regional shortfall". Body: one large horizontal bar chart, regions sorted by revenue change, West's bar in a warm accent and labelled "-£1.2M", the other four in grey. A faint zero line. Source line at the bottom. No bullets (the chart says it), no logo hogging space (a small one in the footer), no legend (one thing is colored and it is labelled).

Three seconds to read. The title alone, in the emailed PDF, still makes the point.

> **Try This**
> Take three slides from a case presentation (or make them). Rewrite each title from a topic to a takeaway sentence. Cut every bullet that repeats the chart. Enlarge the chart to fill the body. Then read just the three new titles and check they tell a coherent mini-story.
