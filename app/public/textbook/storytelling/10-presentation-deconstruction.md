# Case study: real presentation deconstruction

## The one-sentence version

Take a finished data presentation and pull it apart against everything in this book, because reverse-engineering a real deck teaches the craft faster than any checklist.

## What it is

A method for learning from presentations, yours and others':

- Watch or read a data presentation end to end.
- Reconstruct its intended arc: what was the one insight, what was the context, complication, resolution.
- Judge each slide: did the title state a takeaway, was there one clear point, was the chart honest and well-designed, was uncertainty shown.
- Note what worked, what did not, and what you would change.
- Do it to your own decks a week after delivering them, when you can see them cold.

## Why it exists

Principles are abstract until you apply them to a real artifact. A deck that violated three principles and still landed teaches you which principles are load-bearing. A beautiful deck that failed to get a decision teaches you that polish is not persuasion. This chapter is the practice that turns the rest of the book into judgment.

## How it works

**The deconstruction questions, in order:**

**Audience and purpose**

- Who was this for, and what decision was on the table? Can you tell from the deck alone?
- Was the altitude right for that audience (method-heavy for peers, conclusion-first for executives)?

**The one insight**

- What is the single sentence this presentation wanted you to leave with? Is it stated explicitly, on slide 1, in a title?
- Is it a claim with a number, or a vague topic?
- Could you repeat it accurately now?

**Narrative arc**

- Is there a context / complication / resolution structure, or is it a list of charts?
- Does it lead with the answer or build up to it?
- Read only the slide titles. Do they tell the story on their own? Are they takeaways or topics?

**Slide by slide**

- One point per slide, or several competing?
- Chart type correct for the comparison?
- Honest axes? Zero baseline on bars?
- One accent color, or a rainbow?
- Chartjunk (3D, gridlines, legends that could be direct labels)?
- Is there an annotation pointing at the evidence, and a reference line for context?

**Numbers and context**

- Does every number have a comparison (vs target, vs last year, vs peer)?
- Or are there bare numbers with no way to judge if they are good or bad?

**Uncertainty**

- Are estimates given as ranges or as false-precision points?
- Is the load-bearing assumption named?
- Do overlapping confidence intervals get acknowledged, or are near-ties presented as clear wins?

**What was cut**

- Is there an appendix, or was everything crammed into the main flow?
- Did the method get one line and a backup slide, or ten slides up front?

**The outcome**

- Did it get the decision it was asking for? If not, where did it lose the room?
- What is the single change that would have helped most?

**Do it to your own work.** A week or two after you deliver a deck, open it cold and run the questions. You will see the topic-titles you meant to fix, the slide with two points, the number with no comparison, the buildup you promised yourself you would cut. Fix them in your next deck.

## When you use it

As a regular practice. Deconstruct one presentation a week: a conference talk, a public company's investor deck, a colleague's readout, one of your own. Keep a running list of the recurring mistakes you see (and make), and check your next deck against it before sending.

## A worked example

Deconstructing a real-style quarterly readout:

- **Purpose:** unclear from the deck. Turned out to be "approve next year's headcount", but no slide says so, and the ask on the last slide is vague ("we'd like to grow the team").
- **One insight:** buried. The actual finding, "the team's output per head has doubled in 18 months and is now capacity-constrained", appears as a bullet on slide 14. It should be slide 1.
- **Arc:** none. Slides 2 to 13 are a tour of every metric the team tracks, in no particular order.
- **Titles:** all topics. "Ticket Volume", "Cycle Time", "Team Composition". Reading them tells you nothing.
- **Charts:** mostly fine individually, but slide 6 has a truncated bar axis that exaggerates a 5% change, and slide 9 is a dual-axis chart implying a correlation that is an artifact of the scaling.
- **Numbers:** several bare figures. "We closed 1,240 tickets" with no comparison to last quarter or to capacity.
- **Uncertainty:** the headcount ask assumes demand keeps growing at the current rate; that assumption is never stated or tested.
- **Outcome:** the meeting ended with "send us a proposal", i.e. no decision, because the deck never made the case clearly enough to approve on the spot.
- **The one change:** open with "output per head doubled, we are now capacity-constrained, here is the specific ask" and make everything else support that. Same data, a decision instead of a deferral.

> **Try This**
> Deconstruct a completed case presentation (yours or a sample) against the questions above. Write the one insight it should have led with, three slide titles rewritten from topic to takeaway, and the single change that would have most improved it. Then apply those lessons to your Case 20 portfolio piece.
