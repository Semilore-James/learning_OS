# Annotation and context

## The one-sentence version

Numbers and charts do not speak for themselves, so you add the annotation that says what to notice and the context that says whether it is good, bad, big, or normal.

## What it is

Two things layered onto the raw evidence:

- **Annotation** — marks and text on the chart that direct attention: an arrow to the spike, a label on the crossover point, a shaded band for the event that caused the dip, the finding written near the data.
- **Context** — the comparisons that make a number mean something: versus last year, versus target, versus the competitor, versus the rest of the group, versus what the audience expected. A number alone is just a number.

## Why it exists

"Revenue was £4.2M" tells the audience nothing about whether to be pleased or alarmed. "£4.2M, 8% above target and the best quarter in two years" tells them it is good. "£4.2M, down from £5.1M last quarter and the first decline since 2023" tells them it is bad. The number did not change; the context did all the work. And a chart with a visible pattern still needs an annotation to confirm the audience is seeing the pattern you mean.

## How it works

**Give every number a comparison.** When you present a figure, attach at least one of:

- **Versus a target or plan.** "£4.2M against a £3.9M target."
- **Versus a prior period.** "Up 12% year on year." Choose the comparison period honestly (same quarter last year for seasonal data, not the previous quarter).
- **Versus a peer or benchmark.** "Our 3.1% conversion against an industry median of 2.4%."
- **Versus the rest of the group.** "This store is 33% below the next-lowest."
- **Versus expectation.** "We forecast 5% growth; we got 12%."

Without a comparison, a number is not a finding, it is trivia.

**Annotate the chart, do not just describe it verbally.** In a live meeting you narrate, but the emailed PDF has no narrator. Put the finding on the chart:

- The title states the takeaway.
- One or two callouts point at the specific evidence: "Launch, Mar 12" with a line to the inflection; "-40%" at the drop.
- A reference line for the comparison: a faint horizontal line at target or last year's level, labelled.
- A shaded band for a known cause: the promo period, the outage, the price change.

**Explain the "why" where you have it.** A chart shows *what* happened. If you know *why*, say so, on the chart or in the slide's one line of text: "the dip coincides exactly with the checkout bug (fixed Apr 3)". If you do not know why, say that too: "cause not yet identified; three hypotheses in the appendix".

**Context prevents misreading.** A 3% drop looks alarming until you show that the metric routinely swings 5% week to week. A record-high number looks like success until you show it is a record high every quarter because the business is growing and this is just the trend line. The context is what stops the audience drawing a conclusion the data does not support.

**Do not over-annotate.** One or two callouts per chart. If everything is labelled, nothing stands out, and you are back to the audience not knowing what to look at.

## When you use it

On every chart and every number that goes in front of an audience. As you build each slide: what is the one comparison that makes this number meaningful, and what is the one annotation that confirms the audience sees the point.

## A worked example

An analyst shows a line of monthly active users, climbing, and says "engagement is growing".

With annotation and context:

- Title: "Active users up 40% since the onboarding redesign, and holding the gain".
- A shaded vertical band over the two-week rollout, labelled "Onboarding v2".
- A callout at the step-up: "+40% over 4 weeks".
- A faint horizontal reference line at the pre-redesign average, labelled "old baseline".
- One line of text: "The gain has held for three months, so it is not a novelty effect."

Now the audience knows it grew, by how much, from what baseline, because of what, and that it is durable. "Engagement is growing" became a defensible, sized, explained finding.

> **Try This**
> Take a chart from a case analysis. Add: a takeaway title, one reference line for a comparison (target, last year, group average), one callout pointing at the key evidence, and one line stating the cause if you know it. Then check the chart makes its point with the audio muted.
