# Structuring a data narrative

## The one-sentence version

A data narrative moves the audience from a shared starting point, through a complication that demands attention, to a resolution they can act on, and getting that arc right matters more than any individual chart.

## What it is

A structure for the whole presentation, borrowed from how stories work:

- **Context (the setup)** — what everyone already agrees on. "Revenue has grown steadily for two years."
- **Complication (the tension)** — the thing that has changed or that is wrong, the reason we are in this meeting. "But growth has come entirely from existing customers spending more; new customer acquisition has been flat for six months."
- **Resolution (the answer)** — what the data says to do about it. "The acquisition funnel breaks at the trial-to-paid step, which dropped from 22% to 14% after the pricing change. Reverting the trial length recovers most of it."

Context is brief. Complication is where the audience leans in. Resolution is what they leave with.

## Why it exists

A pile of true charts is not persuasive, because the audience does not know which order to think about them in or why they should care. A narrative gives the charts a job: this one establishes the context, this one reveals the complication, these two support the resolution. The structure does the persuading; the charts are evidence within it.

## How it works

**The arc, in slides:**

1. **Title slide = the one insight.** State the conclusion. (See the previous chapter.)
2. **Context (1 slide).** The shared baseline. Keep it short; the audience already knows this. Its only job is to set up the complication.
3. **Complication (1 to 2 slides).** The surprising or concerning finding. This is the emotional center. Spend your best chart here. The audience should feel "we need to deal with this".
4. **Evidence for the resolution (2 to 4 slides).** Each slide is one piece of proof that your recommended action addresses the complication. One idea per slide.
5. **Recommendation (1 slide).** Specific: what to do, who, by when, expected impact, and the confidence range.
6. **The ask (1 slide, for a live meeting).** What you need from this room today: a decision, a resource, a sign-off.
7. **Appendix.** Method, data sources, alternatives considered, detailed caveats. Ready, not shown.

**Two common arcs:**

- **"Things are worse than they look."** Context: the headline metric is fine. Complication: underneath it, something is deteriorating. Resolution: fix it now while the headline still buys time.
- **"Here is an opportunity we are missing."** Context: current state. Complication: a segment or channel is underperforming its potential by a measurable gap. Resolution: the specific move to close the gap and what it is worth.

**Order within the arc:** lead with the conclusion (slide 1), then build the case. Do not save the punchline. Executives who get the answer first can follow the reasoning with the right frame; executives who get 15 slides of buildup are checking email by slide 6.

**One idea per slide.** If a slide needs two sentences to describe what it shows, it is two slides. The title of each slide is a sentence (the takeaway of that slide), and the chart is the evidence for that sentence. Read just the slide titles top to bottom: they should tell the whole story on their own.

**Transitions matter.** "So that is the context. Here is what surprised us." The spoken bridge between sections tells the audience where they are in the arc.

## When you use it

After you have the one insight and before you make slides. Sketch the arc on paper: context bullet, complication bullet, resolution bullets, recommendation. Then each bullet becomes a slide. If a chart does not fit a bullet, it goes in the appendix.

## A worked example

A quarterly business review. The analyst's ten charts, arranged into an arc:

- **Slide 1 (title/insight):** "Q3 revenue hit target, but the mix shifted to lower-margin products and margin fell 3 points; here's why and what to do."
- **Slide 2 (context):** revenue vs target, on plan. One line. Brief.
- **Slide 3 (complication):** margin by month, dropping. The best chart in the deck. "Every product line held its own margin. The blend changed."
- **Slide 4 (evidence):** revenue mix shift, high-margin down, low-margin up, quarter over quarter.
- **Slide 5 (evidence):** the low-margin growth is all one channel (a discount marketplace) that the team leaned into for the revenue target.
- **Slide 6 (evidence):** unit economics of that channel: it hits revenue but loses money after fulfilment.
- **Slide 7 (recommendation):** cap marketplace volume at X, redirect the acquisition spend to the direct channel, expected margin recovery of ~2 points next quarter, confidence moderate.
- **Slide 8 (ask):** sign-off to change the channel targets for Q4.
- **Appendix:** the other four charts, method, the sensitivity on the 2-point estimate.

The slide titles alone tell the story. The charts prove each title.

> **Try This**
> For a completed case, write the arc as three bullets: context, complication, resolution. Then write the slide-title sentence for each slide. Read the titles top to bottom and check they tell the story with no chart visible.
