# Working with the people who asked

## The one-sentence version

The analysis is a conversation with a person who has a decision to make, and how you handle that person matters as much as the SQL.

## What stakeholders actually need

- **A decision they can defend.** Not a data dump, not a dashboard, an answer with a recommendation and a confidence level.
- **To not be surprised later.** If there is a caveat that could flip the conclusion, they need it up front, from you, not from someone in the meeting.
- **Speed calibrated to the stakes.** A directional read this afternoon for a low-stakes call; a defensible number next week for a big one. Ask which they want.
- **To trust you.** Built slowly through consistency, lost instantly by one confident wrong number.

## Managing expectations

**Scope the ask when it lands.** "This is a two-hour question" or "this is a two-week question, here is why" said at the start prevents the disappointment of a thin answer to a request they thought was bigger.

**Give a status before they ask for one.** "Data pulled, cleaning is messier than expected, you will have it tomorrow morning" is a 20-second message that buys you goodwill and a day.

**Under-promise on the timeline.** If you think Thursday, say Friday. Delivering early is a gift; delivering late erodes trust even when the work is good.

## Saying "I do not know yet"

This is a complete, professional answer. "I do not know yet. The sample is too small to call it. If we run it two more weeks we will have the power to." Beats a made-up number every time. Analysts who fake certainty get caught, and then everything they say gets re-checked.

## Pushing back

Sometimes the right move is to challenge the request:

- The question assumes something false ("why did the campaign fail" when it did not fail).
- The data cannot answer it, and no amount of work will change that.
- The requester wants X but needs Y (the XY problem again).

Push back with the alternative attached: "The data can not tell us why churn rose, because we do not track cancellation reasons. What it *can* tell us is which segments the churn concentrated in, which narrows where to look. Want that?"

## Delivering bad news

Lead with the number, not the judgment. "Revenue is 4% below plan, entirely in the West region" is easier to hear than "the West team missed their target again." State the facts, then the likely cause, then what could be done. Keep the temperature low; you are reporting, not accusing.

## The move

Treat every request as the start of a short relationship, not a ticket to close. Confirm the brief, give unprompted status, deliver a little early, say "I do not know" when you do not, and never present a number you have not checked. Do that consistently for a few months and you become the analyst people bring their real problems to.
